Fault root-cause diagnostics

Usually when a fault results in a plant, it takes significant amount of time to identify the root cause. While there may be only one single anomaly signal, there may be many so-called “candidate” root causes.

Meanwhile, a factory is stopped, resulting in massive financial loss. My family business’ experience is a good example that clearly illustrates this problem. In plant related projects, the prime contractor diagnoses the point when there is a process related error and passes the results to the subcontractor responsible for the maintenance. However, if the root cause of the error is not accurately identified before passing on the task, waste is incurred as the subcontractor carries out repetitive maintenance work without actually resolving the root-cause problem, naturally leading to significant waste. Likewise, I realized that root cause diagnosis is a critical process that determines effective maintenance.As such, I wish to leverage advanced statistical modeling that connects process error sources with quality measurements to identify the root causes of variation (Zhou, Chen, & Shi, 2004). More recently, research has even expanded to address optimal sensor placement to maximize diagnostic capability under a limited number of sensors (Liu, Zhang, & Shi, 2014).

I will also focus on deciding which candidate cause to verify first, and what to check next based on the result of the verification. Designing this sequence of inspections is precisely a sequential decision-making problem. By treating dozens of candidate root causes as options and each inspection as an observation, diagnosis becomes a sequential identification problem where the next inspection is selected based on the outcome of each observation, systematically eliminating low-probability candidates. The methodologies I have researched can minimize the required number of inspections while maintaining the desired level of confidence, and supplementing judgments for rarely inspected candidates using existing historical data.

This sequential approach also addresses a concern I encountered in data collection: gathering every possible measurement is costly and often unnecessary. In fault diagnosis, I therefore want to study adaptive data acquisition by drawing on bandit algorithms from online learning and on sequential optimal design to select the most informative inspection at each stage.

As such, I plan to study ways to formulate reliably by utilizing least number of inspections to identify the root cause.

Predictive Maintenance

This problem involves modeling equipment degradation to estimate remaining useful life (RUL) and deciding when to intervene. It is particularly meaningful that the early research began by treating RUL as a distribution rather than a single deterministic number (Gebraeel et al., 2005). Saying "the remaining life is two years on average" could mean it will almost certainly last for two years, or it could also mean that half will last four years while the other half fails within a few months—even with the same mean, these two scenarios represent completely different realities on a plant floor.

The manuscript I am currently co-authoring with my advisor addresses this issue (Sim, J., & Kim, W,2026).Because the mean is heavily skewed by rare extreme values, the option that appears best on average often yields disappointing results in reality. To address this, instead of summarizing the data into a single mean value, we established a new criterion that evaluates which option is more reliable within specific outcome intervals and aggregates these evaluations across the entire distribution. Furthermore, we developed an algorithm and the underlying theory to identify the optimal choice at a desired confidence level based on this criterion. Translated into the “language” of maintenance, our work addresses the problem of selecting a policy that remains consistently reliable across a wide range of real-world scenarios.

However, there is a shortcoming on my end. Until now, I focused mostly on methodologies. In other words, I am yet to experience actual implementation. Although I did work with high-dimensional data, it was mostly static data that was effectively organized (with no time-domain) while a plant’s sensor stream is different—it flows with time, modality gets intermingled, and other factors. As such, I need you to learn to apply to the actual field but by customizing just-right to companies.

Digital Twin

Towards the above-mentioned goal, Digital Twin is a must. A powerful tool, Digital twin is one of the effective ways to simulate changes to adopt in a plant that quantifies uncertainties with simulator and the AI. It is very effective for decision-making (Biller & Biller, 2023). My vision is to create an ecosystem that includes everything from monitoring to diagnostics and maintenance. As such, the digital twin that integrates the entire plant is my goal. In the end, the task of replacing risky work with robotics and other issues such as additive manufacturing are all about what to adopt, when and how to operate.

To support such decisions, however, a digital twin must remain reliable even as the plant and the data it generates continue to change. In practice, equipment deteriorates, operating conditions shift, and sensor data may be limited, noisy, missing, or drawn from distributions different from those used to construct the original model. Therefore, I want to study whether it is possible to design learning and optimization methods that are robust to changes in data distributions while ensuring that near-optimal decisions can be made with limited data. For this purpose, I also need to study different forms of performance guarantees, such as regret bounds and constraint-violation control. Regret bounds can quantify how much performance is lost while the system learns from sequential observations, while constraint-violation control can help prevent unsafe or excessively costly actions during the learning process. As such, I will work with questions such as, “What kinds of learning and optimization methods are needed to maintain stable decision quality in a changing environment?” Ultimately, I hope to develop digital twins that do not merely simulate a static plant, but continuously update their models and support reliable decision-making as real operating conditions evolve.

Smart Factory

Smart Factory (manufacturing) that utilizes IoT, AI, Machine Learning and others is hot these days.

Korea is no exception.

However, we still have a long way to go. According to a report published after the National Assembly (“Congress” of Korea) inspection of the administration (government offices), 79.9% of the smart factories in Korea are at a basic stage, while merely 1.3% enables real-time control.

For the Small and Medium sized Enterprises (SMEs), the picture is grimmer.

This is truly regrettable as 99.0% of all companies in Korea are SMEs, totaling 8 million companies.

As such, I want to focus not only on the large conglomerates, but also the SMEs to introduce small factories to maximize efficiency while minimizing human risk. Accordingly, although I do wish to become an academic/scholar/professor, my other “foot” will always be in the field per se, working very closely with the governments and the companies.

But first, I really need you. For one, I will have to learn the latest technologies to apply. At the same time, however, I know that technological adoption is not easy because not only do we have to factor in the technological functions, but also ROI and manage risks at the same time.

A representative case is my father’s own experience with automatic welders. He did contemplate them, but in the end decided against adoption. Of course, they would offer many benefits like reduced labor cost and so forth. But at the same time, with at least 10 required for a plant of the average size that we build, this would require $12M while automatic welders would need to be customized to individual plant sites, which requires complex time-consuming calibration. And even when adopted, human supervision would be essential all the same.

Thus, I realized that technologies need to be scrutinized in-depth and the benefits and costs (risks, potentials losses included) need to be weighed carefully.

Some processes may be more effective as they are, while some others would need automation through technology adoption. Discerning out which processes need which action is something that I wish to learn. That is, to me, Smart manufacturing may not mean automating every single process down to the bare bone. Instead, although one day, we can dream of 100% automation, until then, strategizing, mixing human labor with technology may be essential. Thus, analyzing which technologies among many to adopt in line with company-specific processes, and which one to maintain the status quo using existing processes is a key challenge especially for the SMEs with limited capital.

That is, transformation from so-called legacy factory to Smart Factory requires extensive decision-making in stages.

Likewise, I will study ways to analyze companies to then identify processes that call for new technologies and others that do not (at least in the short-run).

Moreover, Smart manufacturing goes beyond the boundaries of a plant. It also involves careful supply chain management strategy. And in this process, impeccable data analysis and statistical modeling are essential to cope with uncertainties. The following month's order volume can be forecasted utilizing time series model, but there may be a deviation from the actual demand at any time. Thus, companies must determine inventory levels by considering not only forecast values, but also the costs of overstock and stockouts when demand changes. Stochastic inventory theory addresses inventory policies that minimize costs under such uncertain demand, and the Markov decision process explains how to adjust ordering and production decisions whenever new information arrives over time.

What was interesting while studying these theories is that it is virtually impossible to make good decisions merely with mean demand. That is, optimal inventory level is not determined simply by the mean demand; instead, it must be selected across the demand distribution by weighing the cost of excess inventory against the cost of a stockout. Distributionally robust optimization takes this a step further, seeking to find more resilient decisions under the premise that the assumed demand distribution itself may be inaccurate. This has driven my interest in decision-making models that can function realistically even when data is incomplete and the future is uncertain.

The same principle applies from a temporal perspective, not just a spatial one. A factory requires constant change. This goes for a Smart factory as well. As everything changes such as working environments, facilities must be constantly calibrated to adapt to those changes, precisely why we need continual improvement through monitoring and diagnostics, fault analysis to pinpoint root causes of failure, AS-IS/TO-BE optimization to compare current and target processes, and model-based control to regulate system behavior using data.
